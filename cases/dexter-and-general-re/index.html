Periods: 1993, 1998, 2016 retrospective. Primary records: Berkshire's 2016 letter and the 1998 General Re merger announcement.

Dexter Shoe

Buffett's 2016 letter describes the 1993 Dexter Shoe purchase as a major error. The company cost $434 million, and Berkshire paid with 25,203 shares. The business's value later went to zero; at year-end 2016 the stock issued to sellers would have been worth more than $6 billion. Those are historical retrospective numbers, not today's market values.

The financial lesson goes beyond the failure of one shoe manufacturer. When a successful capital allocator uses its own shares for an acquisition, the foregone future earnings of the shares surrendered are part of the economic price. The apparent deal cost can be far below that eventual opportunity cost.

General Re

Berkshire's June 1998 announcement valued its General Re merger consideration at approximately $22 billion. Buffett's later 2016 reflection highlights the 272,200 Berkshire shares issued and calls the choice to pay with so much stock a major mistake. He does not call General Re worthless: his retrospective separately recognizes its eventual worth as an insurance operation.

That combination illustrates why the evaluation of a target business and the evaluation of what was paid are separate judgments.

A disciplined acquisition scorecard

  • Price the acquirer's shares as ownership interests, not just a quoted exchange rate.
  • Compare the acquired cash-generating asset with the intrinsic value of equity transferred.
  • Model the downside scenario before assuming acquisition synergies.
  • Test the deal against alternatives, including doing nothing.
  • Track per-share outcomes over years, not just the headline transaction size.

Limitations: Quantifying total economic losses requires all transaction-date assumptions, subsequent changes in Berkshire's value and taxes. The documented $6 billion figure is a 2016 historical comparison.
