Periods: 1965 textile takeover, 1967 insurance purchase, 1977 and 1989 reflections.

The original trap

Buffett's 1989 list of mistakes identifies taking control of Berkshire's textile operation in 1965 as an error. He had been attracted by low apparent valuation. The underlying industry's difficult conditions and poor returns on reinvested capital limited the long-term outcome. The 1977 letter also makes clear that management put significant effort into operations and that employment and local obligations complicated decisions.

The retrospective teaches something more useful than “avoid value stocks.” If a weak company needs additional capital just to remain competitive, the investment thesis cannot consist solely of a low initial price.

An alternative capital engine

In early 1967 Berkshire bought National Indemnity Company and National Fire and Marine Insurance Company for approximately $8.6 million. The 1977 letter reports a major expansion in insurance premiums over the ensuing decade, without corresponding issuance of Berkshire shares. The insurance business provided a different path for allocating funds.

Insurance customers supply premium funds before all their claims are settled. Those temporarily investable balances are called float. A reliable and conservatively priced insurance franchise can make the capital valuable to an owner; underpriced policies, catastrophes or reserve errors can make the arrangement dangerous.

Capital-allocation questions

  1. Are profits from existing operations real after maintenance capital and obligations?
  2. Is management reinvesting in the highest-return opportunity, rather than the most familiar one?
  3. Does growth depend on diluting existing owners?
  4. Are apparent free funds matched by obligations whose economic cost is being ignored?
  5. Can the industry absorb price increases and cost shocks?

Limitations: This is a comparison of two Berkshire business engines, not a claim that all textile companies fail or all insurance investments succeed.
